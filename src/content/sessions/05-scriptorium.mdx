> Which copying mistakes can become useful evidence?

**Learning target.** Classify omissions, repetitions and substitutions, and say which are weak evidence.

## Before the workshop

The [print collection](/archive/#print) holds one document you have not read:
the *Ordinance of the Copy-House*, twelve numbered lines of copy-room rules.
You will not read it today either — it stays withheld until week 6.

## Last week, and this week

Week 4 started from one output and asked whether the evidence could tell two
histories apart. This week starts from **five copies and no original**, and asks
a narrower question first: what kind of mistake produced each disagreement
between them?

Classifying the mistake is not the same as ordering the copies — that is next
week's task, done from the table this week produces. Getting the type right
first is what makes the ordering possible at all.

## Check — 5 minutes

Name the three broad classes of copying error this week works with: an
**omission** (something present in the exemplar is missing from the copy), a
**repetition** (something present once is copied twice, or a run is skipped
because the eye jumped between two identical stretches), and a **substitution**
(one reading stands in for another). If you cannot state all three from memory,
say so before the workshop starts rather than during it.

## Receive — 15 minutes

Read the [branching instructions](/packs/week-06/) (the pack folder is shared
with week 6 for the reason above — nothing there is a spoiler). Each of five
teams copies from the previous team's copy rather than from a shared original,
so errors accumulate down a branch instead of each team making independent
mistakes from the same source. Two teams share a parent partway down the
branch; you are not told which two.

## Investigate — 40 minutes

**Produce your witness (20 minutes).** Follow your team's position in the
branching assignment and copy your assigned parent text by hand, line by line,
onto the supplied line-numbered sheet. Introduce at least one deliberate error
of a type your team has not used yet, and log what you changed and why in your
own working notes — this log is not shared yet.

**Classify differences with line IDs (10 minutes).** Compare the five finished
witnesses (`witness-a.txt` through `witness-e.txt` in the [pack](/packs/week-06/))
against each other, not against any original you do not have. At each line
where they disagree, record the line number, what each witness reads, and which
of the three classes in the check above it looks like. `collation.csv` lists the
same disagreement points if your team's hand copies do not turn out to be
informative enough — the instructor fallback exists so that a bad run does not
cost you the exercise.

**Package for another group (10 minutes).** Anonymise your witness labels (swap
them for a scheme the other group has not seen) and hand your table to a
different team. You will reconstruct their pack, not your own, in week 6 — a
team that already knows its own answer would be practising memory, not
inference.

## Evaluate — 30 minutes

Write the error worksheet you are depositing: one row per disagreement, with
line number, the readings, your classified type, and one sentence on how
confident you are in that classification.

Then answer directly: **why might two witnesses agreeing on a spelling
correction be weak evidence that one copied from the other?** Because
regularising an odd or archaic spelling is a change scribes make
unconsciously, and they can make it independently. Two witnesses that agree
only on a normalisation may share nothing more than the same common instinct,
not a common immediate parent. An omission or a repetition is a better signal,
because there is no ordinary mechanism that produces the *same* accidental
loss twice by chance.

## Deposit — 30 minutes

Anonymised witnesses and the classified error table go into the
[print collection](/archive/#print) as `print/ordinance-a` through `-e`. The
true parent log stays withheld until week 6's reveal — this week's deposit is
evidence, not the answer.

## Accessible route

Editable text with line identifiers; no unfamiliar-script skill is assessed.
The plain-text witnesses and `collation.csv` are the task, not a substitute for
it — nothing here requires reading an unfamiliar hand or script.

## Assessment

For [The Stemma](/assessments/the-stemma/), the error types you name today are
the vocabulary its rubric grades in: a shared omission or repetition supports
shared ancestry more than a shared normalisation does, and saying which kind of
agreement you are relying on is part of the mark.

For [The Chain](/assessments/the-chain/), due Friday 2027-03-26 at 12:00
Canberra time, this week's classification skill is not required — but bound
your conclusion there the same way: state what kind of evidence supports it.
