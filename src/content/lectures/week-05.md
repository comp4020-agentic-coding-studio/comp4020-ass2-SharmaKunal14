Three weeks ago you controlled what changed and measured the effect. Last week
you had one output and no history. This hour starts a different kind of
problem: many copies, no original, and a question about **how** each copy
diverged rather than **whether** it did.

## Three ways a hand copy fails

A copyist's eye and hand fail in a small number of recognisable ways, and
naming which one happened is more useful than counting how many differences
there are.

**Omission.** Something present in the exemplar is simply missing from the
copy — a word dropped, a negation lost. An omission that reverses a rule's
sense, rather than merely shortening it, announces itself: the copy stops
making sense on its own terms.

**Repetition and its opposite.** A copyist can write a word or phrase twice
where the exemplar has it once — a **dittography** — or can skip from one
occurrence of a repeated word or phrase to a later occurrence of the same
thing, silently dropping everything between — **haplography**, or, when the
skipped span ends on matching word-endings rather than a matching whole
phrase, **homoeoteleuton**. The direction of these two is usually not in
doubt: an eye skipping ahead over a repeated anchor is a well-attested
failure, and a copyist spontaneously inserting a repeated clause that was
never there is not.

**Substitution.** One reading stands in for another — a synonym, a
misreading, or a deliberate correction. This class is the one that most needs
a second question asked of it, and it is where the week's check comes from.

## The reading that lies to you

Not every substitution is scribal error in the sense the previous three are.
The commonest substitution is quiet **normalisation** — regularising an
archaic idiom or an odd spelling into its more familiar form — and it is a
change scribes make **unconsciously and independently**. Two witnesses that
agree only on a normalisation may share nothing more than the same ordinary
instinct toward tidiness. That agreement looks exactly like shared inheritance
and is not: it is what a **polygenetic** change looks like, one that entered
more than once because nothing about producing it depends on having a
particular ancestor.

Contrast that with a haplography, or an omission that breaks the sense of the
line it is in. Neither has an ordinary mechanism for happening twice, by
different copyists, in exactly the same place, in exactly the same way. A
reading like that is the kind of evidence that actually locates a shared
ancestor.

**The rule the week hands you.** A shared error is strong evidence for shared
ancestry in proportion to how implausible it is that two copyists made it
independently. A shared normalisation is weak evidence for exactly that
reason: it is entirely plausible that two copyists made it independently.

## Anonymised, and withheld on purpose

Wednesday's five witnesses disagree with each other in several places, and
none of you can see the document they all come from — the Ordinance stays in
the print collection's withheld half until week 6's reveal. That is
deliberate: reconstructing a text you already know how to read is a memory
exercise, and this course is not testing memory.

Your job this week stops at classification: what kind of mistake happened at
each disagreement, and how confident that classification is. Week 6 is where
the table you produce gets turned into a claim about who copied whom.
