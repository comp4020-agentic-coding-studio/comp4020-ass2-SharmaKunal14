Last week you measured one copy at a time against a source. This week two copies
share something, and the question is what that entitles you to say.

## Where this comes from

Through the 1970s and 80s, cassette duplication carried most of the recorded
music in South Asia, largely outside the industry that thought it owned the
business. Small operators dubbed from whatever they had, which was usually
somebody else's dub.

*From Peter Manuel,* Cassette Culture *(University of Chicago Press, 1993),
introduction and chapter 3. No linkable copy exists, so the claim used here is
stated rather than quoted: the technology's reach came from being cheap and
uncontrolled, and the fidelity cost was accepted rather than solved.*

The historical point is not the subject of this hour. It is that a population of
copies existed with no records of what came from what — which is the situation
this course is about, and which is exactly when you have to read the objects
instead.

## The worked example

Two copies, W and X. Both carry a raised noise floor. Does that mean they are
related?

No. Every analogue dub raises the noise floor. If the source lacks it and all
four copies have it, the feature separates copies from the source and separates
nothing among the copies. It is a property of the process, not of any particular
history.

Now: two copies, X and Y, both carry a 40-millisecond dropout in the left channel
at 00:12.400.

That is a different kind of thing. It is a single physical event at a fixed
location, and for two dubs to produce the same flaw, the same length, the same
channel, the same timestamp, independently, would be a coincidence nobody should
accept without argument. The economical explanation is that both were dubbed from
a copy that already had it.

## Four explanations, one question

Whenever two copies share a feature, four explanations are available, and
ancestry is only the last of them.

**It is in the source.** The most common and the most easily forgotten. If the
original recording has a door closing at 00:04.900, every faithful copy has it,
and two copies sharing it tells you only that both were copied from this source.
Rule this out first, every time.

**Every copy acquired it separately, because the process does that.** Noise
floor, wow and flutter, generation loss in general. Shared by everything, so
diagnostic of nothing.

**They were made on the same equipment.** This is the one worth slowing down for.
Frequency response is a property of a machine and its settings. Two dubs made on
the same worn deck will share a rolloff above 12 kHz whether or not they have
anything to do with each other. **Shared equipment is not shared ancestry**, and
it produces exactly the pattern that shared ancestry produces: a feature in some
copies and not others.

**They descend from a copy that already had it.** Only now.

## What makes the difference

Not rarity, exactly. The property you want is that **independent occurrence is
implausible** — that the feature is specific enough, and arbitrary enough, that
producing it twice by accident would be a coincidence.

A one-off flaw at a fixed timestamp has that property. A frequency response
curve does not, because there is a mechanism — the same deck — that produces it
twice without any relationship at all.

This is a claim about mechanisms, not about statistics. When you propose a
relationship on Wednesday, you are asserting that you cannot think of a plausible
mechanism that would produce the feature twice independently. State that
assumption, because someone may think of one.

## What this week does not do

You are pairing copies, not building a tree. With four copies and one diagnostic
feature you can support one relationship; you cannot say how many copies sat
between, or in what order, or whether the intermediate still exists.

Week 6 builds the tree. This week earns one edge of it.
