# The original that never was

Weeks 4-11 treated the surviving evidence as given. It never was —
someone spent a budget deciding what would survive. This week makes
that decision visible.

---

## An aura is not a file format

Benjamin (1936): even a perfect copy is missing the original's
"presence in time and space" — its aura.

Read literally against a JPEG, that sounds like nostalgia. Read
correctly: a reproduction can carry an object's *content* while
dropping everything about how it came to exist.

Week 11 showed this exactly — same image data, two dropped facts.

## Why source-tier costs money

The image family's entire source file: **0.0109 GB** — a few cents a
year to store.

Auditing, describing and making provenance accessible is a **per-item**
cost. It doesn't shrink because the file is small.

$120/year across three families cannot buy full audited provenance for
everything.

---

## Toy Story 2, 1998

A routine command deleted ~90% of the film's working files. The tape
backups everyone trusted had been silently failing for a month.

The film survived because **Galyn Susman** had a personal copy on a
home computer — reconciled file-by-file against the salvage.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

## Two lessons from an accident

An untested backup is not evidence of preservation — only an
unverified claim of it. Same distinction as week 11's record vs.
signature.

A budget states what an archive **commits** to keep. It says nothing
about what survives anyway, by luck, outside the plan.

---

## Two proposals, same $120

**Deep image, thin everything else** — full source-tier for the image
family. **$104.005** total, naive value **38**.

**Start preserving real audio** — source-tier for 4 of 5 audio items,
the archive's first real audio bytes. **$118.010** total, naive value
**40**.

Neither dominates. Image polishes an already-known-incomplete record;
audio creates coverage where none exists.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## The response refuses to average it away

A memo has to state which consideration it weights higher — not
collapse both questions into one number.

---

## One rate change flips it

Source-tier labor: $12 → $20/item (a per-item audit, per week 11).

"Deep image" → **$120.005** — tips over budget.

"Start preserving real audio" → **$105.003** — falls further under it.

The same $8/item change moves them in opposite directions: they spend
on families of very different sizes.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: Walter Benjamin's 1936 essay
argues a perfect copy still lacks the original's "presence in time and
space," its aura — read literally against a JPEG that sounds like
nostalgia, but Benjamin's real point is that a reproduction can carry an
object's content while dropping everything about how it came to exist,
exactly what week 11 showed when a manifest carried the same image data
while dropping two facts an audit could have used. Preserving a family at
source tier is expensive not because storing bytes costs money — the
entire image family's source file is 0.0109 GB, a few cents a year — but
because auditing and describing each item's provenance is a per-item cost
that does not shrink with file size, so a $120-a-year budget across three
families cannot buy full audited provenance for everything. Caleb
Stephens's account of the 1998 near-loss of Toy Story 2, where a routine
command deleted roughly 90% of the film's working files and the assumed
tape backups turned out to have been silently failing for a month, shows
this tradeoff going wrong by accident: the film survived only because
Galyn Susman happened to have kept a personal copy, which teaches two
things — an untested backup is only an unverified claim of preservation,
the same distinction week 11 drew between a record and a signature, and a
budget states what an archive commits to keep, saying nothing about what
survives anyway by luck. This week's pack gives two proposals for the same
$120 budget: "Deep image, thin everything else" brings the image family to
full source-tier preservation for $104.005 with naive value 38, while
"Start preserving real audio" brings four of five audio items to source
tier — the archive's first real audio bytes — for $118.010 with naive
value 40; neither dominates, because spending on image polishes an already
incomplete record while spending on audio creates coverage where none
exists, and the response text refuses to resolve that by averaging the two
considerations into one number. When source-tier labor rises from $12 to

$120.005 and tips over budget while "Start preserving real audio" falls to
$105.003 and stays comfortably under it — the same $8-per-item change
moves the two proposals in opposite directions because they spend their
source-tier dollars on families of very different sizes, and checking that
required rerunning the arithmetic rather than re-reading the proposal.
