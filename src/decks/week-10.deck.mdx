# Enhance

Week 4 asked whether evidence on a single output could tell two
histories apart. This week: when someone shows a "before" and an
"after" and calls the after a recovery of lost detail — what would
actually verify that, and what would only make it look verified?

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Three claims that are not the same claim

**Plausible.** Looks like a coherent photograph. Weakest claim, easiest
to satisfy.

**Verified.** The restored value at a named location matches a retained
source. Strong — and requires exactly what a real restoration usually
lacks: the original.

**Uncertain, not fabricated.** A flat, never-at-risk region matching
tells you nothing about the method's power.

---

## This week's pack, stated plainly

An 8×8 synthetic tone chart, degraded by 2×2 block averaging, restored by
bilinear interpolation.

Checked at six named locations: two uniform-block features matched
(uninformative). Of four features with genuine variation: **one matched,
three did not.**

One out of four is not a recovery rate — it's what this pack's own
arithmetic produced.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

## A documented failure at higher stakes

PULSE (Menon et al., CVPR 2020) searches a generative model's space of
plausible faces for one that downsamples to match a blurry input.

A pixelated photo of Barack Obama went in. White skin, blue eyes came
out — verifiably wrong, because a real photo exists to check against.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## The correct general lesson

Not "generative restoration invents things," as a blanket rule.

**Plausibility was never evidence of correctness** — and checking
requires exactly the retained source a real restoration claim usually
cannot supply.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: three claims about a
restoration are easily confused but not the same — plausible (looks like
a coherent image, the weakest and easiest claim), verified (a restored
value at a specific named location matches a retained source you can
check against), and uncertain-not-fabricated (a region that was never at
risk, such as a flat area, matching the source tells you nothing about
the restoration method's power, since flat areas survive almost any
degradation and restoration). This week's pack degrades an 8×8 synthetic
tone chart by averaging each 2×2 block and restores it with ordinary
bilinear interpolation, then checks six named locations against the
retained original: two features sat in already-uniform blocks and matched
uninformatively, while of four features with genuine internal variation,
only one matched and three did not — one correct recovery out of four
genuine attempts in this one specimen with this one method, not a general
recovery rate. The same structure appears at much higher stakes in PULSE
(Menon et al., CVPR 2020), a generative face-restoration method that,
when run on a pixelated photo of Barack Obama, produced a plausible face
with white skin and blue eyes — verifiably wrong because a real photograph
exists to check against. The correct general lesson is not that generative
restoration always invents detail, but that a plausible-looking output was
never evidence of correctness on its own; checking requires exactly the
retained source that a real restoration claim, unlike this pack's, usually
cannot supply.
