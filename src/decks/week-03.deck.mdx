# What the ear cannot hear

Week 2 ended with a proposed explanation. This hour is about the
difference between proposing one and testing it.

---

## Loss designed to be inaudible

Perceptual coding does not try to keep the signal. It keeps the parts a
model of the listener says will be noticed, and discards the rest.

*From Jonathan Sterne,* MP3: The Meaning of a Format *(2012), chapter 1.*

A codec is an argument about hearing, built into a file format.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## A comparison that cannot answer it

> One encoding cycle at 320 kbps: deviation score 0.3.
> Ten encoding cycles at 96 kbps: 9.8.
> Conclusion: ten encoding cycles cause a large loss.

The numbers are fine. The conclusion does not follow.

---

## Two things changed

Cycle count: 1 → 10.

Bitrate: 320 → 96.

There is no way to divide the 9.5-point difference between them. This is
not a subtle error — it is everywhere, because the two conditions were
probably never designed as a comparison at all.

---

## The repair

Change one thing. Hold everything else fixed.

> One cycle at 128 kbps versus ten cycles at 128 kbps.

Bitrate constant; only the cycle count differs. Wednesday's data: **3.2
points** — roughly a third of what the confounded comparison implied.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Four things a protocol must state

- The **independent variable**
- The **outcome measure**
- The **control**
- **What was held fixed**

Leave any one out and a reader cannot tell what your result is about.

---

## The number that matters most

You are reporting a *difference* between two conditions, so what governs
you is how much that difference moves when the whole procedure repeats.

For us: **0.5 points.**

---

## A non-detection is a finding

Run the corrected comparison at 320 kbps: **0.4 points.**

That is at or below repeatability — a **non-detection**, not a small
difference. Reporting it as degradation would be reporting your own noise.

"We did not detect a difference" and "there is no difference" are
different claims. Only the first one is yours.

---

## Where that leaves the question

At 128 kbps: detectable. At 320 kbps: not.

A worse headline than "generation loss ruins everything," and a better
answer — conditional on a stated source, score, setting, scenario. Not a
claim about MP3 in general.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

## The control, again

Ten copy operations, no encoder: scores zero.

A clean control does not prove the procedure is sound. It rules out one
specific failure — that the procedure manufactures differences regardless
of treatment. Run it first.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: perceptual audio coding
discards whatever a model of the listener predicts will go unnoticed, so a
codec encodes a theory about hearing. A supplied comparison claims ten
re-encoding cycles at 96 kbps cause a 9.5-point-worse score than one cycle
at 320 kbps, but the comparison changes two variables at once — the cycle
count and the bitrate — so the difference cannot be attributed to either
one. Correcting it by holding the bitrate fixed at 128 kbps and varying
only the cycle count gives a smaller, attributable difference of 3.2
points. The repeatability of that kind of difference, re-measured, is 0.5
points, which matters because running the same corrected comparison at 320
kbps gives 0.4 points — a non-detection, meaning this procedure cannot
tell whether the two conditions differ at all, not evidence that they are
identical. A protocol has to state its independent variable, outcome
measure, control and what was held fixed, or a reader cannot tell what the
result is actually about; the exact-digital-copy control, run through the
same number of operations and scoring zero, checks the apparatus rather
than proving the procedure sound.
