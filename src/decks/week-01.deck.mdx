# The office that ate itself

Sixty minutes on what a copy is, before we spend twelve weeks arguing about
what happened to one.

---

## Chester Carlson's problem

Carlson demonstrated electrophotography in 1938. More than twenty companies
declined to license it over the next decade.

The recorded objection was not that it failed. It was that carbon paper
already existed, and nobody was asking for anything else.

*From David Owen,* Copies in Seconds *(2004), chapter 1.*

## Copy room test card 0S1I-B85l

Four lines, built from characters that look like one another:

`0`/`O` · `1`/`l`/`I` · `5`/`S` · `8`/`B` · `2`/`Z` · `6`/`G` · `9`/`g`

A legibility card concentrates the failure so it can be counted.

---

## One measurement, position by position

```
card   The clerk in Room 108 filed 1,051 slips before noon.
copy   The clerk in Room 1O8 filed 1,05l slips before noon.
```

Two positions differ: a `0` read as `O`, a `1` read as `l`.

Every transcription is the same length as the card — no alignment judgement,
just a count.

---

## The rule matters more than the number

A rule you can hand to someone else, who then gets **your** number, is a
measurement.

A number you produced by looking carefully is an impression.

Wednesday's counting rule is the first thing this course asks you to trust
someone else to reproduce.

## Four copies, one control

| Copy | Channel | Operations |
|---|---|---:|
| Digital control | digital | 8 |
| Photocopy | photocopy | 1 |
| Photocopy | photocopy | 4 |
| Photocopy | photocopy | 8 |

The control runs the **same eight operations** as the longest photocopy
chain. Not once — eight, checksummed at every step.

---

## What the control buys

**Eight photocopy operations are worse than one. Eight digital operations are
not worse at all.**

A digital copy made *once*, set beside a photocopy chain of eight, would
prove nothing — the obvious objection is that it was never tested.

Matching the operation count is what makes this a control instead of a
gesture.

---

## The finding, stated precisely

This copying **process** damages what it copies.

Copying **as such** does not.

That distinction is the whole reason week 1 opens with a control rather than
a dramatic chain of ruined pages.

## Two readers, one copy, one disagreement

After a single photocopy operation, reader R1 records an error at "Room 108".
Reader R2 records none.

Both followed the same transcription procedure.

---

## Three claims that are not the same claim

- The **counting algorithm is deterministic** — same transcription, same
  card, same number, every time.
- **Your own repeatability** is a separate check: count one transcription
  twice.
- **Readers still differ**, because resolving a damaged mark is an
  interpretation, not a lookup.

---

## Legibility is a relation, not a property

Reader R1 scores *below* R2 after four operations and *above* R2 after eight.

The readers cannot even be ranked consistently.

An honest table reports that spread. Averaging it away would have destroyed
the one thing worth knowing.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: Chester Carlson's photocopier
took a decade to be adopted because the problem it solved wasn't one anyone
had asked about. This course's copy room test card 0S1I-B85l is built from
confusable character pairs so a legibility failure can be counted exactly —
compare a transcription to the card position by position, since both are
the same length. A digital control, copied byte for byte eight times and
checksummed at each step, comes back identical, while a photocopy chain of
the same length degrades — showing the damage belongs to this photocopier,
not to copying in general. Separately, two readers transcribing the same
single photocopy already disagree at one position, which is not a flaw in
the counting rule (deterministic, by construction) but evidence that
legibility is a relation between a copy and a reader, not a fixed property
of the copy.
