# The scriptorium

Three weeks ago you controlled what changed. Last week you had one
output and no history. This hour starts a different kind of problem: many
copies, no original, and a question about **how** each copy diverged.

---

## Three ways a hand copy fails

**Omission.** Something present in the exemplar is missing from the copy.

**Repetition and its opposite.** Dittography (written twice); haplography
(the eye skips ahead, dropping what's between).

**Substitution.** One reading stands in for another.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

## The reading that lies to you

Not every substitution is scribal error in the same sense.

**Normalisation** — regularising an archaic idiom into its familiar form —
is a change scribes make **unconsciously and independently**.

Two witnesses agreeing on a normalisation may share nothing but the same
ordinary instinct toward tidiness.

---

## Polygenetic vs. inherited

A normalisation is **polygenetic** — it entered more than once because
nothing about producing it depends on a particular ancestor.

A haplography, or an omission that breaks the line's sense, has no
ordinary mechanism for happening twice, independently, in the same place.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## The rule the week hands you

A shared error is strong evidence for shared ancestry in proportion to
how implausible it is that two copyists made it independently.

A shared normalisation is weak evidence for exactly that reason.

---

## Anonymised, and withheld on purpose

Five witnesses disagree with each other in several places.

None of you can see the document they all come from — the *Ordinance*
stays withheld until week 6's reveal.

Reconstructing a text you already know is a memory exercise. This course
is not testing memory.

What kind of mistake happened at each disagreement, and how confident is

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: hand copying fails in a small
number of recognisable ways — an omission drops something present in the
source; a dittography repeats a word or phrase that appeared once; a
haplography skips ahead from one occurrence of a repeated element to a
later one, silently dropping what lies between; and a substitution
replaces one reading with another. Substitutions need a second question
asked of them, because the commonest kind, normalisation — regularising an
archaic or odd spelling into its familiar form — is a change scribes make
unconsciously and independently of each other, so two witnesses agreeing
only on a normalisation may share nothing more than the same ordinary
instinct rather than a common ancestor: this is called a polygenetic
change, one with no mechanism tying its recurrence to shared descent. A
haplography or a sense-breaking omission has no such easy mechanism for
happening twice by chance, which is why a shared error of that kind is
strong evidence for shared ancestry in proportion to how implausible
independent occurrence would be, while a shared normalisation is weak
evidence for the identical reason. This week's task stops at classifying
each disagreement among five anonymised witnesses by type and confidence;
turning that classification into a claim about which witness copied from
which is deliberately left for week 6.
