# When ancestry becomes hard to read

Week 6 built a tree from shared copying error in a handful of witnesses.
This week asks a narrower question: what happens to that method as the
error rate rises — inside a model simple enough to state completely.

---

## The model, stated completely

A 24-symbol sequence, four-symbol alphabet, is the fixed ancestor.

Each generation: at every position, independently, with fixed probability
(the error rate), swap in a different symbol; otherwise leave it alone.

One lineage. No branching, no death, no differential survival.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Ancestor agreement

The fraction of positions in generation *g* that still match
**generation 0** — not the immediate parent.

It measures how much of the original signal a reconstruction attempt
would still have to work with.

---

## Three rates, three seeds — not one dramatic run

Three fixed error rates. Three fixed seeds each.

A single run would show a shape; it wouldn't show whether that shape is a
property of the rate or an accident of one random sequence.

Anyone can rerun `settings.json` and get the same nine sequences back.

---

## What a finite run can and cannot show

Agreement never falls to zero — with four symbols per position, an
**unrelated** sequence still matches by chance about a quarter of the
time. Every curve settles toward ~0.25, not 0.

A single seed can tick *up* between two generations, even while the
three-seed mean keeps falling.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

## What the model does not have

- **Selection** — no variant is more likely to persist than another
- **Population structure** — one lineage, no drift, no bottleneck

Three rates and three seeds cannot locate a general error-rate threshold,
in this model or any other, and say nothing about a real biological
sequence.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: this week's model copies a
fixed 24-symbol sequence over a four-symbol alphabet forward through
generations, where each position independently has a fixed probability of
being replaced at each step; "ancestor agreement" is the fraction of
positions still matching the original generation-0 ancestor, not the
immediate parent. The pack runs this at three error rates with three
random seeds each, which matters because a single run at a single rate
shows a shape but cannot show whether that shape reflects the rate itself
or one random sequence of substitutions — the three seeds at a given rate
can disagree with each other by more than the gap between two rates' means
at an early generation. Two things follow directly from the model and
neither is a biological claim: agreement never reaches zero, because an
unrelated four-symbol sequence still matches the ancestor at roughly a
quarter of positions purely by chance, so every curve settles toward about
0.25; and a single seed's curve is not smooth, since a substitution can
restore a match by chance as easily as break one. What the model excludes
— selection between variants and any population structure such as
branching or drift — means this simulation cannot locate a general
error-rate threshold for readable ancestry, in this model or any real one,
and says nothing about actual biological sequences.
