# The curse of recursion

Week 7 asked what a finite run of copying error can and cannot show about
ancestry. This week: what happens when each generation is a fresh sample
drawn *from* the previous one — and what that does to the cases that
were already rare.

---

## The claim this model illustrates

Shumailov et al., "AI models collapse when trained on recursively
generated data," *Nature* 631 (2024).

**Early collapse** — drift from the original distribution, before any
category disappears.

**Late collapse** — a rare case is assigned zero probability and never
recurs.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Six categories, one fixed at 3%

Category F: fixed at 3% true probability. Round 0 draws 200 samples.

**Baseline** — keeps drawing from the true distribution (the control).

**Fully recursive** — estimates each round from the previous round's own
draw.

**Retain-original** — estimates from a 50/50 mix of prior draw + fresh
true-distribution draw.

---

## What happened, across three seeds

Fully recursive: one seed sent category F to **zero by round 3**, and it
stayed zero — once an estimate assigns zero probability, sampling from it
can never recover the category.

Baseline: never lost it, in any seed.

Retain-original: didn't lose it, in any of the three seeds run here.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

## What three seeds do and don't show

They show the mechanism is real and reproducible in this model.

They do **not** show how often it happens, how fast, or whether a 50/50
mix specifically is what a real pipeline would need.

---

## Before this gets over-applied

No language model, no token vocabulary, no training objective here.

This model shows *that* recursive re-estimation from a finite sample can
permanently erase a rare case — a mechanism general enough to plausibly
matter elsewhere. It does not show that any particular real model
degrades this way, or at what rate.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: this week's toy model has six
labelled categories, with category F fixed at a 3% true probability, and
compares three conditions across 200-sample rounds — a baseline that
always draws from the true distribution (the control), a fully recursive
condition that estimates each round's distribution purely from the
previous round's own sample, and a retain-original condition that mixes
the previous round's sample 50/50 with a fresh true-distribution sample.
Across three seeds, the fully recursive condition drove category F to a
count of zero for one seed by round 3, and it never recovered — once an
estimated distribution assigns a category zero probability, sampling from
that estimate cannot reintroduce it — while the baseline never lost the
category in any seed and the retain-original condition did not lose it in
any of the three seeds tested. Three seeds are enough to show the
mechanism is real and reproducible in this toy setting, but not enough to
establish a rate, a timeline, or whether a 50/50 retention ratio
specifically is what a real training pipeline would need; the model has no
architecture, tokens or training objective, so it demonstrates that
recursive re-estimation from a finite sample can permanently erase a rare
case, not that any particular real generative model degrades this way or
at what speed.
