# Hiss as a signature

Last week you measured one copy against a source. This week two copies
share something, and the question is what that entitles you to say.

---

## A population with no records

Through the 1970s and 80s, cassette duplication carried most of the
recorded music in South Asia, outside the industry that thought it owned
the business. Small operators dubbed from whatever they had — usually
somebody else's dub.

*From Peter Manuel,* Cassette Culture *(1993), introduction and chapter 3.*

## Two copies, W and X

Both carry a raised noise floor.

**Related?**

No. Every analogue dub raises the noise floor. A property of the process,
not of any particular history.

---

## Two copies, X and Y

Both carry a 40ms dropout, left channel, at 00:12.400.

A single physical event at a fixed location. Two dubs producing it
independently would be a coincidence nobody should accept without
argument.

## Four explanations for a shared feature

1. **It is in the source.** Rule this out first, every time.
2. **Every copy acquired it separately**, because the process does that.
3. **Same equipment.** Shared equipment is not shared ancestry.
4. **They descend from a copy that already had it.** Only now.

---

## The one worth slowing down for

Frequency response is a property of a machine and its settings.

Two dubs made on the same worn deck will share a rolloff above 12 kHz
whether or not they have anything to do with each other.

It produces exactly the pattern shared ancestry produces.

---

## What makes the difference

Not rarity, exactly.

**Independent occurrence is implausible** — the feature is specific and
arbitrary enough that producing it twice by accident would be a
coincidence.

A frequency-response curve does not have that property; a one-off dropout
at a fixed timestamp does.

---

## A claim about mechanisms, not statistics

Proposing a relationship asserts that you cannot think of a plausible
mechanism that would produce the feature twice independently.

State that assumption. Someone may think of one.

## What this week does not do

You are pairing copies, not building a tree.

With four copies and one diagnostic feature you can support one
relationship — not how many copies sat between, in what order, or whether
the intermediate still exists.

Week 6 builds the tree. This week earns one edge of it.

## Text walkthrough

For a reader who cannot use the deck view: cassette duplication in South
Asia through the 1970s and 80s produced a population of copies with no
record of what came from what, which is the situation this course reads
objects instead of records for. Whenever two copies share a feature, four
explanations compete: it may already be in the source, every copy may have
acquired it separately because the copying process does that to everything,
both copies may have been made on the same equipment (a shared deck
produces a shared frequency-response rolloff without any shared ancestry
at all), or the copies may genuinely descend from an intermediate that
already had it. A raised noise floor shared by two copies fails all but the
second explanation. A 40-millisecond dropout in one channel at one
timestamp, shared by two copies, is different: it is a single event at a
fixed location, and independent occurrence in two separate dubs would be an
implausible coincidence, which is what makes it evidence for a shared
ancestor rather than for the process or the equipment. With four copies and
one such feature this only supports pairing two of them — it says nothing
about how many intermediates sat between them, which week 6's larger
witness set is needed to address.
